Players are ranked by their average finish across the heroes they play, each finish weighted by how much it's worth trusting: a deeper pool counts for more (#1 of 20 over #1 of 3, saturating around 7 opponents — past that, deeper fields in this group are not stronger ones), and a hero you've actually played counts for more than one that only just cleared the display floor. Finishing position itself is never rewritten by pool size — 7th of 36 is 7th of 36. That average is then pulled toward the middle of the board by how much evidence backs it, so breadth and volume decide who leads when two records are close — but a thin record is only ever held back from the top, never pushed below a worse one. The Avg finish chip on each profile is the raw, unadjusted figure. The standing itself is an exact place — players who score equally are separated by their average finish, so no two share one. A player carrying less than one fully-credible hero's worth of evidence is shown with a — instead of a place: not enough to rank them, which is not the same as a low rank.

How Style fit, Mastery and Profile work — and what Style fit predicts

For each hero they play we ask where they sit among the tracked players of that hero, over the role's core output, kill and survival stats (accuracy is excluded — it isn't comparable across heroes). Those per-hero readings are averaged, weighted toward the heroes they've played most, into one style shape. Measuring inside each hero's own pool is what keeps the answer about them: playing more of one hero no longer changes what we say about a different one.

A hero's archetype is half its rivalsmeta benchmark shape and half the shape this group actually plays it in — two independent readings of the same thing, so a benchmark refresh moves the answer half as far. Archetypes are read against the Season 10 benchmark snapshot (2026-09-26), so these are season-relative numbers.

What this predicts, in numbers (docs/HERO_FIT_VALIDATION_2026-07-26.md): tracked across 265 real hero pickups, a closer pre-pickup style match predicted modestly stronger lobby-relative stats on the new hero — rho +0.13 (95% CI +0.01…+0.25), worth roughly a quarter of a rank sub-division. On win rate it showed no measurable effect (rho −0.07; any benefit is bounded at about 1.4pp between the best- and worst-fit thirds) — a descriptor, not a recommender.

Mastery (style) is how close their output on those same fit axes sits to that hero's benchmark (100% = matches it, capped 150%); the full-formula proximity is in Hero standings above. Each hero's benchmark covers a different slice of its leaderboard — the whole qualifying field on some heroes, its top 500 on others — and that hero's page names which.

Profile reads the Fit×Mastery corner for heroes they play. Mastery here is relative to their own heroes in this role, not to an absolute bar: half of anyone's heroes sit below their own median by definition, so a lower-half hero can still be at or above that hero's benchmark. Read these as where a hero sits among their others, never as a verdict on how good they are at it.

  2. Upside — suits their style; they get less out of it than their other heroes here (which may still be a lot)
  3. Maverick — works against their style, yet they get more out of it than most of their others

A — in Profile means they don't play that hero at all. Every hero they do play gets one of the four.

With only two heroes in a role the split is one each — the higher of the pair counts as their stronger side however close the two are, so read those rows as an ordering, not a gap.

Profile is a soft read (two noisy numbers, split at the player's own median) — weakest on thin pools, and on any row whose fit is grey: there the split line sits inside the error bar, so the tag is printed in grey too rather than in its own color. Fits are drawn from a centre line at zero — right (green) for a fit, left (red) for an anti-fit, and grey for the ones that can't be told apart from 0. Length is always the size of the fit, so a grey bar can be every bit as long as a colored one. A fit keeps its color once it reaches about 0.84× its own ± — the point where its sign is roughly 80% likely to be right, the same separability test used everywhere else on the site — so a wide ± can leave a large fit grey while a smaller one beside it stays colored. The ± on each fit is measured two ways at once: how much the number moves when any one of their heroes is dropped, and how far the two archetype readings disagree. It covers most of what a benchmark refresh does to these numbers over a week or two, and less of what a full season does — over a month, expect movement beyond the bars.

How a trophy is won, and what it is not

A trophy is awarded for holding a hero's crown when a season ends, and is kept for good. It is the same crown the Leaderboard and the roster board show — the multi-method consensus winner over that hero's pool, not the Power Score table alone.

It does not stack. One hero is one trophy however long it was held, so a case counts heroes — and every plaque says how many season ends it took, on its pedestal or, where the run was short, on the plate itself. Those ends need not be consecutive: a crown lost and taken back adds to the same trophy. Losing the crown never takes the trophy back — that is what the silver rim means, and why a case can be larger than the Crowns count beside it.

A dashed + S10 chip is not a trophy. It marks a crown held right now, in a season that has not ended — it banks only if the crown is still theirs when it does.

Holding now, nothing banked is a third shelf, and those plaques are not trophies either — no season has yet ended with that crown theirs, so there is nothing to bank. They are drawn unstruck (flat and dashed, never gold or silver) and carry no pedestal. The shelf exists because a hero whose crown recently changed hands otherwise showed only silver plaques, and a case full of silver reads as a title nobody holds. A role sweep — every hero's crown in one role at once — rides the same shelf, drawn as a cut stone rather than a disc because it outranks the plaques beside it. None has ever banked, for anyone, at any of these season ends.

These boards are reconstructed, not recorded. This project did not exist for most of S0–S9.5, so every one of the 19 season ends is rebuilt by scoring each player's career-to-date at that date under today's weights — one yardstick for all 19, at the cost of being a reconstruction. The result is then frozen: a later re-tune cannot rewrite who won what.

Each plate names how many players the holder finished ahead of in that hero's pool, and the hover gives the size of the field. Pool size is not a bar — whoever the board would have crowned at that season end has the trophy — so a hero only one player has taken past the game floor is banked too, and its plate says uncontested rather than claiming a field it never had. The one bar is that floor: a trophy can only be won in a pool the board would have shown. Quick Match and Competitive are banked separately — different fields, different game floors (25 here), and very often different holders.

Best duo partners

Lift is what the pair is worth: their win rate together minus the rate the two of them manage apart — a strong partner is not automatically a good duo. Partners with 15+ games together are ranked on it, each with ± its own error; treat anything inside that as even. Everyone else is collapsed below, on profile Fit alone. Click any scored column to sort; #, Partner and They add are labels, not rankings.

How Lift and Fit are computed

Lift is the same number the Teammates axis prints for the same duo: the pair's rate minus the mean of what each of them does in games without the other (each side shrunk for sample size), then shrunk again by the pair's own sample. It is descriptive, not causal — duos also choose when and against whom they queue.

Fit is the profile read, and it is asked from this player's side: Coverage weights each archetype axis by how far they are from the benchmark on it, so it scores what a partner covers for them rather than how good that partner is; Independence is low hero overlap (no fighting over picks). Real chemistry is small: once coin-flip noise is subtracted, the spread of true lift across this roster's pairs is about 2.0 points, so a lift only moves Fit in proportion to how much of it could be real — a 15-game pair keeps 2% of its lift, a 250-game pair 28%, a 1,000-game pair 61%.

The tested table is ranked by Lift, not by Fit — Fit has already absorbed whatever part of that lift could be real, on top of the profile read, so a partner further down the table can still show the higher Fit. That is the two columns answering different questions, not a mis-sort.

Record book

Personal TIMELINE milestones from the group's stored match history (QM + Competitive pooled), on one shared date axis — when each of these happened is the read the Hall of Fame's single-game leaderboards can't give a single player. The store is a floor of the full career: the axis starts where the crawl does, so a mark against that left edge may have a bigger twin just off it.

Their single-game personal bests (most KOs, damage or healing in one match) live on the Hall of Fame axis, not here — View in Hall of Fame.

How “best” is decided

The best day and the best stretch rank by win rate, not by win count, and that rate is shrunk toward 50% for small samples — so a 3-0 afternoon does not outrank a 17-6 marathon. A day or window under 3 games is not eligible at all: a 1-2 game day is a coin-flip, not a personal record. A winning day or window therefore always outranks a losing one — but a player with none on record still gets a best of what there is, so one of these can print a losing W–L. The W–L printed is always the real, unshrunk record.

Streaks are consecutive stored matches with no calendar gap rule — a run that spans two nights, or a months-long break in play, is still one streak, which is why several of these carry a date range rather than a single date. On the timeline that width is calendar time, not playing time: a streak straddling a break draws wide without being a long run of games.

Under competitive pressure

Change in output vs the same benchmark as Hero standings (percentage points), from Quick Match to Competitive — positive = sharper under pressure. Judge each hero against its role, not zero — the roster-typical delta is Vanguard +3.1 · Duelist -0.3 · Strategist +3.1.

How this comparison is built

Season-matched: both sides of every comparison are the same season of play (15+ games in each mode in that season), compared over the stats recorded in both modes — so this reads how they hold up, not when they played. A player qualifies on those matched seasons alone — they don't need a separate Quick Match standings profile to appear here.

Output vs benchmark is weighted by Competitive games and fades a thin comp sample toward no change, so a hot streak can't read as clutch; the per-hero cards below are the unfaded deltas with their Competitive game counts and the two stats that moved each one most.

Standing in the lobby is a second, independent read of the same question that cannot drift with the era at all — standing among the same-role players in each lobby, Competitive minus Quick Match, centred so 0 = the roster-typical competitive drop. It is never averaged into the first: the two measure different things, so a clash between them is a result, not an error to be smoothed away.

Both are moves in percentage points — of output against the benchmark, where 100 is the benchmark itself, and of standing in the lobby, where 100 is ahead of every same-role player in it — which is why they can share one axis: bar lengths are comparable, not just their directions. The axis is the same on every player's page.

Coordinated Competitive lobbies punish dive KO-farming while Quick Match baselines include the mode's disguised bot lobbies, so the same player's raw delta is role-confounded before anything else happens to it — hence the roster-typical figures above, and the role-adjusted verdict.
