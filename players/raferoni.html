Players are ranked by their average finish across the heroes they play, each finish weighted by how much it's worth trusting: a deeper pool counts for more (#1 of 20 over #1 of 3, saturating around 7 opponents — past that, deeper fields in this group are not stronger ones), and a hero you've actually played counts for more than one that only just cleared the display floor. Finishing position itself is never rewritten by pool size — 7th of 36 is 7th of 36. That average is then pulled toward the middle of the board by how much evidence backs it, so breadth and volume decide who leads when two records are close — but a thin record is only ever held back from the top, never pushed below a worse one. The Avg finish chip on each profile is the raw, unadjusted figure. The standing itself is an exact place — players who score equally are separated by their average finish, so no two share one. A player carrying less than one fully-credible hero's worth of evidence is shown with a — instead of a place: not enough to rank them, which is not the same as a low rank.

How a trophy is won, and what it is not

A trophy is awarded for holding a hero's crown when a season ends, and is kept for good. It is the same crown the Leaderboard and the roster board show — the multi-method consensus winner over that hero's pool, not the Power Score table alone.

It does not stack. One hero is one trophy however long it was held, so a case counts heroes — and every plaque's plate says how many season ends it took. Those ends need not be consecutive: a crown lost and taken back adds to the same trophy. Losing the crown never takes the trophy back — that is what the silver rim means, and why a case can be larger than the Crowns count beside it.

Every crown on Still holds is theirs right now, in S10.5 — a season that has not ended. S10.5 is added to a trophy only if the crown is still theirs when it does.

Holding now, nothing banked is a third shelf, and those plaques are not trophies either — no season has yet ended with that crown theirs, so there is nothing to bank. They are drawn unstruck (flat and dashed, never gold or silver) and name no tenure. The shelf exists because a hero whose crown recently changed hands otherwise showed only silver plaques, and a case full of silver reads as a title nobody holds.

A role sweep — every hero's crown in one role, held at once — is the rarest thing a case can hold, so it heads the case instead of standing on a shelf: a cut galaxy-opal stone in a gold setting, with the role's heroes beside it. It banks like a trophy, at a season end, and is never taken back; one held now with nothing banked is drawn dashed and purple, like the shelf for crowns in the same state.

These boards are reconstructed, not recorded. This project did not exist for most of S0–S10, so every one of the 20 season ends is rebuilt by scoring each player's career-to-date at that date under today's weights — one yardstick for all 20, at the cost of being a reconstruction. The result is then frozen: a later re-tune cannot rewrite who won what.

Each plate names how many players the holder finished ahead of in that hero's pool, and the hover gives the size of the field. Pool size is not a bar — whoever the board would have crowned at that season end has the trophy — so a hero only one player has taken past the game floor is banked too, and its plate says uncontested rather than claiming a field it never had. The one bar is that floor: a trophy can only be won in a pool the board would have shown. Quick Match and Competitive are banked separately — different fields, different game floors (50 here), and very often different holders.

What these columns measure

Place in group is their place among the tracked players on the hero; the bar fills to the share of the others they finish above, so a place reads the same in a group of 4 or of 38, and the table sorts on it. Uncontested means they are the only tracked player on it.

vs everyone on file scores their numbers on the hero against everyone on file with an hour or more on it, weighted the way the hero's Power Score is: 50 is typical, 90 is near the top. It is the same reading as Player DNA, summed up per hero.

Win rate is adjusted for luck the way the hero page's Win rate tab is — pulled toward the group's rate on the hero, more for fewer games — and is coloured only when it clears chance. Last 10 is their last ten stored matches on the hero, oldest to newest, with how long ago when the newest is over a month old.

Projected rank and Last 10 do not change with the mode selector (the projection is the hero ladders' Quick Match + Competitive blend, and Last 10 pools the stored matches of both); the other columns are this mode's.

Style match — within role

How this player's numbers lean next to everyone else on the same heroes, and which heroes are played the same way — style, not skill. A closer match went with slightly stronger stats on that hero and with no measurable change in win rate: a description, not a promise of more wins.

How Style match works — and what it predicts

Their style. For each hero they play, their healing, assists, damage, KOs and deaths per minute — and damage taken, on Vanguards — are compared with every other player on file on that same hero. The percentages are those comparisons averaged across their heroes, with more weight on the heroes they have more games on. Comparing inside each hero keeps it about them: playing a damage-heavy hero doesn't make anyone look damage-heavy.

The match. Every hero leans too: how everyone on file plays it next to the other heroes in its role — a big-healing, low-damage healer, say. Style fit is how closely the player's lean and the hero's point the same way: +100 the same lean, 0 unrelated, below 0 the opposite. It compares lean, not level, so it reads style rather than skill. Shared lean on a card names the stats doing most of the matching. Measured against 1,031,196 players on file, each in the mode the games were played in.

What this predicts, in numbers (docs/STYLE_MATCH_REBUILD_2026-10-08.md): over 505 heroes the tracked players play, each hero's match — worked out without that hero — went with slightly stronger lobby-relative stats on it (rho +0.07, 95% CI −0.02…+0.15; the method it replaced scored +0.00 on the same test). On win rate it showed no measurable effect (rho +0.02, 95% CI −0.06…+0.12) — a descriptor, not a recommender.

Mastery (style) is how close their output on those same stats sits to that hero's benchmark (100% = matches it, capped 150%); the full-formula proximity is in Hero standings above. Each hero's benchmark covers a different slice of its leaderboard — the whole qualifying field on some heroes, its top 500 on others — and that hero's page names which.

Profile reads the Fit×Mastery corner for heroes they play. Mastery here is relative to their own heroes in this role, not to an absolute bar: half of anyone's heroes sit below their own median by definition, so a lower-half hero can still be at or above that hero's benchmark. Read these as where a hero sits among their others, never as a verdict on how good they are at it.

  1. Signature — suits their style, and among the heroes they get the most out of here
  2. Upside — suits their style; they get less out of it than their other heroes here (which may still be a lot)
  3. Maverick — works against their style, yet they get more out of it than most of their others
  4. Uphill — works against their style, and they get less out of it than their other heroes here

A — in Profile means they don't play that hero at all. Every hero they do play gets one of the four.

With only two heroes in a role the split is one each — the higher of the pair counts as their stronger side however close the two are, so read those rows as an ordering, not a gap.

Profile is a soft read (two noisy numbers, split at the player's own median) — weakest on thin samples, and on any row whose fit is grey: there the split line sits inside the error bar, so the tag is printed in grey too rather than in its own color. Fits are drawn from a centre line at zero — right (green) for a fit, left (red) for an anti-fit, and grey for the ones that can't be told apart from 0. Length is always the size of the fit, so a grey bar can be every bit as long as a colored one. A fit keeps its color once it reaches about 0.84× its own ± — the point where its sign is roughly 80% likely to be right, the same separability test used everywhere else on the site — so a wide ± can leave a large fit grey while a smaller one beside it stays colored. The ± on each fit is how far it moves when any one of their heroes is left out — the same check that greys a match score on a card.

Player DNA — by role

Where this player ranks on each stat among everyone on file who plays the same heroes — 50th is typical, 90th is the top 10%.

How DNA is measured

vs everyone on file ranks each stat, hero by hero, against every player on file with an hour or more on that hero, in the same mode: on this player's heroes, 30 to 196 players a hero, 47% of them tracked players. The games on file are the tracked players' own matches, so few others reach an hour on one hero. A few games on a hero count for less: those numbers are pulled toward the hero's typical rate first, so a short sample reads close to typical. The role's figure averages their heroes, weighted toward the ones they have more games on; pick a hero above the chart to read it on its own, or open a stat to see it hero by hero.

vs the group places them among the tracked players who play each hero: on every stat the group's best scores 100 and its lowest 0, and the role's figure averages their heroes the same way. With only a few tracked players on a hero, 100 just means the best of those few — pick a hero to see their place, like 2nd of 8.

Survivability is deaths: fewer deaths rank higher. On Vanguards, Damage Blocked is the damage they soak, where more is the job. Accuracy shows only for the heroes that score it, and Crit rate — the share of their hits that crit — only for the heroes whose attacks can crit, like Luna Snow.

How this is built

A dot's area is that hero's matches in the season. The solid dot, and the portrait in the Main row, is the hero they spent the most time on that season; a line joins back-to-back seasons on the same main, and the longest run as main counts seasons they played (one sat out does not end it; of two equally long runs, the later is shown). Peak rank is the account's best Competitive rank that season, in both views. S10.5 is still being played.

How a trophy is won, and what it is not

A trophy is awarded for holding a hero's crown when a season ends, and is kept for good. It is the same crown the Leaderboard and the roster board show — the multi-method consensus winner over that hero's pool, not the Power Score table alone.

It does not stack. One hero is one trophy however long it was held, so a case counts heroes — and every plaque's plate says how many season ends it took. Those ends need not be consecutive: a crown lost and taken back adds to the same trophy. Losing the crown never takes the trophy back — that is what the silver rim means, and why a case can be larger than the Crowns count beside it.

Every crown on Still holds is theirs right now, in S10.5 — a season that has not ended. S10.5 is added to a trophy only if the crown is still theirs when it does.

Holding now, nothing banked is a third shelf, and those plaques are not trophies either — no season has yet ended with that crown theirs, so there is nothing to bank. They are drawn unstruck (flat and dashed, never gold or silver) and name no tenure. The shelf exists because a hero whose crown recently changed hands otherwise showed only silver plaques, and a case full of silver reads as a title nobody holds.

A role sweep — every hero's crown in one role, held at once — is the rarest thing a case can hold, so it heads the case instead of standing on a shelf: a cut galaxy-opal stone in a gold setting, with the role's heroes beside it. It banks like a trophy, at a season end, and is never taken back; one held now with nothing banked is drawn dashed and purple, like the shelf for crowns in the same state.

These boards are reconstructed, not recorded. This project did not exist for most of S0–S10, so every one of the 20 season ends is rebuilt by scoring each player's career-to-date at that date under today's weights — one yardstick for all 20, at the cost of being a reconstruction. The result is then frozen: a later re-tune cannot rewrite who won what.

Each plate names how many players the holder finished ahead of in that hero's pool, and the hover gives the size of the field. Pool size is not a bar — whoever the board would have crowned at that season end has the trophy — so a hero only one player has taken past the game floor is banked too, and its plate says uncontested rather than claiming a field it never had. The one bar is that floor: a trophy can only be won in a pool the board would have shown. Quick Match and Competitive are banked separately — different fields, different game floors (25 here), and very often different holders.

Player DNA — by role

How DNA is measured

vs everyone on file ranks each stat, hero by hero, against every player on file with an hour or more on that hero, in the same mode: on this player's heroes, 41 to 66 players a hero, 61% of them tracked players. The games on file are the tracked players' own matches, so few others reach an hour on one hero. A few games on a hero count for less: those numbers are pulled toward the hero's typical rate first, so a short sample reads close to typical. The role's figure averages their heroes, weighted toward the ones they have more games on; pick a hero above the chart to read it on its own, or open a stat to see it hero by hero.

vs the group places them among the tracked players who play each hero: on every stat the group's best scores 100 and its lowest 0, and the role's figure averages their heroes the same way. With only a few tracked players on a hero, 100 just means the best of those few — pick a hero to see their place, like 2nd of 8.

Survivability is deaths: fewer deaths rank higher. On Vanguards, Damage Blocked is the damage they soak, where more is the job. Accuracy shows only for the heroes that score it, and Crit rate — the share of their hits that crit — only for the heroes whose attacks can crit, like Luna Snow.

How Duo partners works

Most-played partners are the 4 tracked players they have the most games with on the same team. Each card shows the hero each of the two plays most in their games together, this player's first, with its share of those games; how long they have played together; and a bar per season for how much.

Together vs apart is the duo's win rate minus the average of what each of them wins in games without the other (each record adjusted for its size), pulled toward zero by the duo's own sample — the same Lift the Teammates page prints for the pair. The band is its 95% range: about two standard errors (the ± in the table) either side. Hover or tap a duo for its numbers. It is descriptive, not causal: duos also choose when, and against whom, they queue.

A dot turns green or red only when the gap survives a false-discovery test across this player's list, and the badge above the chart names those duos; purple means it can't be told from even. With many partners a band or two will miss even by chance alone, so the colour comes from that test, not from the band. Real chemistry is small here — about ±1.1 points across every pair — while a 200-game duo carries about ±3.5 points of error, so most duos play even. That is the data, not a fault.

How these are counted

Team MVP rate: the share of their games in which they were their team's best — MVP on a winning team, SVP on a losing one, one award per six-player team. Par is the group's award rate on the same heroes, each weighted by their games on it, with their own games left out. Award rates run from about 3% to 34% from hero to hero (Strategists ~11% vs Duelists ~16% pooled), which is why par follows the heroes they play. The gap is coloured only when it is beyond chance (a 5% false-discovery rate across the group); purple means inside it.

Mid-match hero swaps: a game counts when they played two or more heroes in it; “bringing in” is the role they switched to most.

Games a week: their stored games between the first and the last — a floor of the true count, and how much they play, not how well.

Game-to-game consistency: how much their place in the lobby moves from one game to the next — the spread of their per-game standings after each hero is centred on their own average on it, so a hero swap is not an inconsistent game. The band is the group's thirds, with both names when the read sits too close to a cut to tell. How “best” is decided

The best day and the best stretch rank by win rate, not by win count, and that rate is shrunk toward 50% for small samples — so a 3-0 afternoon does not outrank a 17-6 marathon. A day or window under 3 games is not eligible at all: a 1-2 game day is a coin-flip, not a personal record. A winning day or window therefore always outranks a losing one — but a player with none on record still gets a best of what there is, so one of these can print a losing W–L. The W–L printed is always the real, unshrunk record.

Streaks are consecutive stored matches with no calendar gap rule — a run that spans two nights, or a months-long break in play, is still one streak, which is why several of these carry a date range rather than a single date. On the timeline that width is calendar time, not playing time: a streak straddling a break draws wide without being a long run of games.

How the expectation is built

An enemy counts as the hero they played most in that match.

A bare rate here is not a personal read, so every cell is re-based. The expectation is their own win rate in their matches without that hero, shifted by how much better or worse the squad does facing it than with it absent — counted team by team, with every one of their own matches taken out (leave-one-out by match — nobody is measured against their own games). A match with that hero on their own team counts in none of it. A hero qualifies at ≥15 encounters.

The printed % is raw; the ±pp beside it is the gap to that expectation. How this comparison is built

Season-matched: both sides of every comparison are the same season of play (15+ games in each mode in that season), compared over the stats recorded in both modes — so this reads how they hold up, not when they played. A player qualifies on those matched seasons alone — they don't need a separate Quick Match standings profile to appear here.

Output vs benchmark is weighted by Competitive games and fades a thin comp sample toward no change, so a hot streak can't read as clutch; the per-hero cards below are the unfaded deltas with their Competitive game counts and the two stats that moved each one most.

Standing in the lobby is a second, independent read of the same question that cannot drift with the era at all — standing among the same-role players in each lobby, Competitive minus Quick Match, centred so 0 = the roster-typical competitive drop. It is never averaged into the first: the two measure different things, so a clash between them is a result, not an error to be smoothed away.

Both are moves in percentage points — of output against the benchmark, where 100 is the benchmark itself, and of standing in the lobby, where 100 is ahead of every same-role player in it — which is why they can share one axis: bar lengths are comparable, not just their directions. The axis is the same on every player's page.

Coordinated Competitive lobbies punish dive KO-farming while Quick Match baselines include the mode's disguised bot lobbies, so the same player's raw delta is role-confounded before anything else happens to it — hence the roster-typical figures above, and the role-adjusted verdict.
