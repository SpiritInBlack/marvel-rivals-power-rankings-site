How the gap is built

The gap is each map's raw record minus the pooled rate in the last column's heading, both at one decimal, and its ± is one standard error — counting that the pool holds the map's own games. No map is pulled toward the pool, and the reason is the answer the rows give: fitted over every hero's maps, the pull they support is worth about 5,200 games, so a 500-game map would keep only 9% of its distance from that pool, and the typical row here, 802 games, 13%. Across the whole site, map-to-map differences for a hero are mostly luck, so a pulled column would shrink nearly every row to a fraction of its gap. So the record shows as it is, and the bar below decides which gap, on this table, is more than luck.

These rows come from a wider population than this hero's ranked tables will use. A gap here is only ever measured against the rate its own rows produced.

The bar a gap must clear scales with the table, not just with the row. A grey gap is not a small gap; it is one that did not clear the bar this table's whole set of comparisons is held to — a stricter test than looking at that row on its own. How the side read is built

Which games. Quick Match on Convoy and Convergence maps only: one round, so a team plays one side all match. The match record never says which side a team was on, but it shows: the second team only ever wins on the clock, so the first team is attacking. Competitive swaps sides inside a match, and Domination has no sides, so neither counts here. Every player counts, not just ours; a match with this hero on both teams is left out, because one of the two always wins.

Expected is what the average hero wins on that side, on the same maps in the same season, with every match this hero played left out. Each game adds its own map's rate, because maps move the side edge far more than heroes do — attackers win about a third of games on one map and well over half on another — and patches move it too.

The lean is the read. It is attack's distance from expected minus defense's, so the hero's own level cancels out: a strong hero beats the average on both sides, and that is not a lean. The ± beside each number is one margin of error, counting the expected rates' own uncertainty as well as this hero's games. The lean is coloured only past 1.96 of them, the bar a single comparison needs, and the line drawn around it spans that bar, so a line that crosses the centre is inside chance. Across the 56 heroes with a side read, about 3 would clear that bar by luck alone; 18 do.

How Lift is computed — and what it finds

Lift is the pair's win rate minus what the two heroes are worth apart: this hero's win rate in its matches without that ally, plus how far that ally wins above 50% in its matches without this hero. Each team counts once, and a match with either hero on both teams is left out, so no pairing is scored against its own games. The gap is then pulled toward zero by the pairing's own sample — a 20-game run keeps 67% of its gap, a 2,000-game one 99.5%. That is a nudge, not a guard: a lucky short run can still top this table, and the ± beside it is what says so. That ± carries the error in all three rates, not just the pair's: the two without rates are counted in games too, so their own uncertainty belongs in the band and is in it.

Why not just read Win %: it answers which teammate heroes are strong, not which pairings work. Remove one number per ally hero — the same number on all 56 heroes — and most of that order goes with it. On this build a page's Win % order agrees with that ally's own rate elsewhere at a mean rank correlation of +0.80; its Lift order, -0.04.

There is real chemistry here, and it is small. Across all 3,074 tested pairings on 56 heroes, a Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery pass at 5% returns 44. 13.2% of rows sit beyond ±2 of their own error where chance alone gives 4.6%, and the spread of the whole table's z scores is 1.35 against the 1.00 that pure noise produces. Once that noise is subtracted, the spread of real chemistry across the table is about 1.3 points — most pairings move the win rate by about that much either way, and only the highlighted rows are told apart from luck. Run it hero by hero instead — 56 smaller tests, so a lower bar — and 73 rows across 32 of the 56 heroes clear it, where 56 independent 5% tests would flag about 3 heroes if nothing were there.

So a highlighted row is a finding, and the rest of the ranking is a shortlist of where to look. Anything inside its own ± is even.

How “expected” is built

Expected is this hero's win rate in its matches without that enemy, shifted by that enemy's own edge — how far above or below 50% it wins in its matches without this hero, reversed. Each team counts once, and a match with either hero on both teams is left out, so no row is scored against its own result. That shift is a point or two, not a rate: each cell's hover prints the rate it started from and the shift applied to it. vs expected is Win % minus Expected — the difference of the two numbers printed beside it, both at one decimal — with its margin of error (one standard error) beside it. It takes a colour only when it survives a false-discovery test over this table's own rows. A pointer-only tooltip on each vs expected gap adds the same difference in standard errors, the scale on which a thin row and a deep one are comparable.

Rows under 5 games are omitted; a match counts once per enemy hero it fields against this one, over every player in the games we hold. A match with this hero on both teams is left out — one of the two always wins, so it says nothing about the hero — which is why the table has no row for it. Descriptive, not causal: enemy picks track map, mode and rank band, so a low row says this hero tends to lose the lobbies that enemy shows up in, not that the enemy counters it.
