How it's calculated

Each method takes the SAME tracked stats and rescales each stat 0–100 across the players shown, by a different rule; a player's score in a column is then the weighted blend of their rescaled stats. So the endpoints below (a 0, a 100, an exact step) are what a method does to a single statistic — a blend of several rarely lands on them — and the same player can land on a different number under each method. (This hero's inverted scored stat — Deaths — is flipped first, so "better" always means a higher score; look for the ↓ in the Scored stats tab.)

  1. Min-Max. Stretches the field so the lowest raw value becomes 0 and the highest becomes 100, with everyone else placed proportionally in between. Simple and intuitive, and the most sensitive of the three to how far apart the raw numbers actually are. One guard: in a pool of 12 or more, an endpoint sitting more than 50% of the pool's range away from its nearest neighbour is clamped onto the scale instead of defining it — otherwise that single value would flatten everyone else toward the middle — so it can end up sharing 0 (or 100) with the next player along.
  2. Z-Score. Measures how many standard deviations each player sits from the group's average, then maps that onto the 0–100 scale (50 = dead average). Resistant to outliers — one standout doesn't crush everyone else toward 0 the way it does under Min-Max — but that also means Z-Score rarely reaches the ends of the scale; an evenly-matched group clusters closer to the middle than Min-Max would show it.
  3. Percentile. Ranks players by raw value and converts each rank into "beat X% of the field" (ties share the average of their positions). Cares only about ORDER, not by how much: neighbouring players sit exactly 100 ÷ (players − 1) apart however close or far their raw numbers are — a 33-point step in a 4-player pool, under 3 points in a 36-player one.

The three are not three independent juries. All of them rescale the SAME stats, and Min-Max and Z-Score both read magnitude — on the current pools they produce the identical ranking on about six views in ten. Only Percentile, which reads order alone, is a structurally different question, so “unanimous” means the three rules agree, not that three independent measurements did.

No outright sweep — the three methods don't all name a single #1; the winner has the best average rank. The three bars share one 0–100 track on purpose, so a method that compresses the field (Z-Score, which centres on 50 and rarely reaches either end) draws shorter bars than one that stretches it to 0 and 100 — read bar lengths down a column, not across one; a score under 8 is drawn at 8 so a last place stays visible. (#) is the player's rank under that method. The group winner has the best average rank; players tied on average rank are ordered by total Power Score (the sum of the three method scores) — the = beside the crown means this one was.

How to read the bars

A bar is a standing, not the number beside it. Each statistic is rescaled 0–100 across the players shown — the group's best on that column fills the track, the group's worst sits at the floor below — and the bar draws that standing. It is what makes columns in different units comparable at a glance, and it is why a long bar means ahead of these players and never good in absolute terms: change the pool and every bar in the table moves. The Power column is the exception. Its bar is the composite score itself, drawn on a fixed 0–100 track rather than rescaled across the players shown — so unlike every other column here, the group's best Power bar fills the track only if that player actually scores 100, and on most panels it stops well short of the end.

The shortest bars are floored. A standing under 8 is drawn at 8 — and so is a Power score under 8, the one bar here that is not a standing — so a last place reads as a bar rather than a sliver, which means the shortest bars in a column can look level while the numbers beside them differ. Where two bars look the same length, the numbers are the answer.

Where the weights come from. Rescaling makes the statistics comparable; it does not make them equal. Each carries a fixed weight, set for this hero when its formula was calibrated — the composition bar at the top of this panel is those weights, drawn to scale and labelled with each share, with the calibration date beneath it. The Power column is exactly those weights applied to the standings in this table, which is why a player who leads more columns can still sit lower: the ones they lead may be the light ones.

How to read the radar

The distance along a spoke is a 0–100 standing, never the raw number. The rim is the reference: 100 is the best value anyone in this group posts on that spoke, 0 is the worst, and the faint rings in between mark standings of 25, 50 and 75 — hover a vertex for the standing it actually holds. The centre is not quite zero: the whole 0–100 range is drawn into the outer 92% of the radius, so a player at the bottom of a spoke still shows a short arm instead of collapsing onto the middle. Because that is a rescale and not a cut-off, two low standings keep different lengths here even where the floored bars in the stat tables draw them level.

Statistics where fewer is better are turned around before they are plotted, so further out is better on every spoke whichever direction the statistic runs in. That is why the radar carries none of the ↓ marks the sortable columns do: the direction has already been taken out of the number being drawn.

Outlines rather than filled shapes, deliberately. A filled polygon reads as area, and area is not the Power Score — it counts every spoke alike and squares each one, while the score weights them (the formula bar at the top of this panel is those weights) and adds them straight. So a bigger outline is not always the better player.

One player in front. The best-ranked player drawn gets a heavier line with a dot on every spoke, and everyone else drawn stays a thin, lighter line behind them, so the one to read stands out by weight and not by colour alone. Click a name in the legend and that player comes to the front instead. On a pool of three or more, the dotted grey outline is the group median: outside it on a spoke means ahead of half the group there.

A game counts once per player, for the hero they played longest that match and the team-up they had on it — a hero they swapped to for a minute takes no game, as everywhere on this site — and a win is that player's team winning the match. The partner counts as on the team when a teammate played them as their main hero. Bot lobbies and event modes are excluded, as everywhere on this page.

A card needs 30 games on a team-up to earn its bar and a player's cell 10; under that the rate still prints, greyed, with its wins–losses beside it, so a thin record reads as a thin record rather than vanishing. Neither rate is adjusted or ranked — these are records, and a team-up's population rate is mostly a fact about who chooses it. The line under the cards calls one team-up the winner only once the gap between them is about twice its margin of error; short of that the two are level. Every win-rate bar here is drawn on the same window around a coin flip as the map and teammate tables — the bar follows the printed rate, it just spends its width where win rates actually land. vs pool prints its margin of error beside it, and takes a colour only once the gap is larger than a family of that many comparisons could produce by luck — a muted bar is a real gap that is still inside the noise.

The enhanced bonus. “With” against “without” the partner is exact, but it mixes the team-up's enhanced effect with everything else the partner brings to a team. So each card also works out what the partner is worth when this team-up is not equipped — from the hero's other team-up — and adds that to this team-up's own rate without them: the rate to expect if the enhancement did nothing. The bonus is how far the actual rate beats it, ± one standard error, printed once all four records behind it reach 30 games. Averaged over every hero it comes to +4.5 pp in quick match (±1.1) and +3.8 pp in competitive (±2.7): the enhancement does win games, even where a single team-up's record is too thin to show it.

Projected competitive rank — Emma Frost

Projected Emma Frost rank — an estimate from how each player performs, not their live account rank. Take the order seriously at a distance rather than row by row: players a few places apart are a real difference, neighbouring rows usually sit inside the model's own margin, and the III/II/I sub-divisions are low-precision.

How are these ranks estimated?

The estimate deliberately ignores each player's real competitive rank — a rank can sit above or below true skill — and reads how they actually play instead. Three ingredients:

  1. A baseline from everything they play. Across all of a player's heroes, how much do they produce compared with the top of each hero's leaderboard? That sets their typical level — a hero with hundreds of games shapes it far more than a two-game cameo.
  2. This hero, against their own lobbies. In the group's recorded matches, does the player out-perform the other players in the same role in the same match — by more than a typical pilot of this hero does? Some kits out-stat a lobby just by existing (sustained-farm damage, high-uptime healing) and some under-stat it (dive, burst, utility), so each hero's lobby standing is judged against that hero's own typical number — the “typical for this hero” percentage in the row, taken from the seasons the player played it (patches move it). Beating that pushes the hero above your baseline; falling short pushes it below. Competitive and Quick Match both count — the matchmaker pairs you against your own level either way — and recent games weigh more (a gentle half-life of 100 games), so growth shows without having to drag up a whole career's weight.
  3. A reality check. A hero can't read much higher than what the player actually puts up on it — beating an easy lobby on a hero you produce little on doesn't lift the tier. The check trusts itself in proportion to its evidence: a deep career's numbers cap the read almost absolutely, while a thin 15-game record only pulls it about halfway down (a handful of games shouldn't overrule hundreds of recorded lobbies). Reads held down this way are marked output-capped.

The confidence pill says how much match data backs the read on this hero: HIGH = a deep dominance sample, MED = a moderate one (hover it for the exact credibility score). Nobody's own Account Peak positions their own row — a player can read above it or below it, and where they land is decided entirely by the three ingredients above. Recorded ranks do set the board's height, once, for everyone: because output-vs-benchmark runs systematically generous, the whole board is shifted so the median player lands on the median account peak. That is a ruler, not an anchor — it moves every row by the same amount and changes nobody's position in the order. Scores turn into tiers on the game's own scale: Bronze III starts at 3000, and every division is 100 points.

Where this read runs hot. The lobby comparison is only as strong as the lobbies, and nothing here subtracts how tough they were — a player who mostly meets weak ones can rest a tier or more above anything their account has actually reached. That is what the Account Peak column is for: a projection sitting far above a peak is the row to be sceptical of, not one the model has verified.

One all-time projection per player, not one per view: Quick Match and Competitive games both count, so this board covers every player shown in either view above — which can be more than either view lists on its own — and does not change when you switch modes.

One number, two awards. Par is leave-one-out. How Lift is computed — and what it finds

Lift is the cell's win rate minus what the two heroes are worth apart: the group's rate over every teammate pairing it has ever recorded, plus how far this hero runs above that rate without that ally, plus how far that ally runs above it alongside every other hero. Each term leaves this pairing's own games out, and the gap is then pulled toward zero by the pairing's own sample — a 20-game run keeps 67% of its gap, a 2,000-game one 99.5%. That is a nudge, not a guard: a lucky short run can still top this table, and the ± beside it is what says so. That ± carries the error in the expectation as well as in the cell: the ally column is a few thousand games rather than a whole career, so its own uncertainty belongs in the band and is in it.

Why not just read Win %: it answers which teammate heroes are strong, not which pairings work. Remove one number per ally hero — the same number on all 56 heroes — and no measurable chemistry is left. On this build a page's Win % order agrees with that ally's own rate elsewhere at a mean rank correlation of +0.49; its Lift order, +0.01.

And the honest answer is that there is very little here. Across all 2,891 tested pairings on 56 tracked heroes, a Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery pass at 5% returns none. 4.9% of rows sit beyond ±2 of their own error where chance alone gives 4.6%, and the spread of the whole table's z scores is 1.02 against the 1.00 that pure noise produces. Once that noise is subtracted, the spread of real chemistry across the table is about 0.6 points — small enough that a simulated table built with zero chemistry in it reproduces the figure. Run it hero by hero instead — 56 smaller tests, so a lower bar — and 5 rows across 4 of the 56 heroes clear it, which is what 56 independent 5% tests turn up when nothing is there (about 3 heroes).

So the ranking is a shortlist of where to look, not a list of findings. Anything inside its own ± is even.

How “expected” is built

Expected is this table's own games-weighted average, shifted by how much better or worse the roster does against that enemy hero than it does overall — with this hero's own games taken back out (leave-one-out — no hero is scored against its own contribution). That shift is a point or two, not a rate: each cell's hover prints the average it started from and the shift applied to it. vs expected is Win % minus Expected — the difference of the two numbers printed beside it, both at one decimal. A pointer-only tooltip on each vs expected gap adds the same difference in standard errors, the scale on which a thin row and a deep one are comparable.

Rows under 5 games are omitted; a match counts once per enemy it contributes, per tracked player fielding this hero — so a stacked lobby is counted several times. That inflates the games and correlates the rows without biasing the rates; the rail below is the check, since correlated rows widen the pile and its width is printed there. Mirror rows are real — the enemy team can field this same hero. Descriptive, not causal: enemy picks track map, mode and rank band, so a low row says the group tends to lose lobbies that hero shows up in, not that the hero counters this one.
