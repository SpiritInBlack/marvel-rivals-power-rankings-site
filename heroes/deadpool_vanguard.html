How are these three methods calculated?

Each method takes the SAME tracked stats and rescales each stat 0–100 across the players shown, by a different rule; a player's score in a column is then the weighted blend of their rescaled stats. So the endpoints below (a 0, a 100, an exact step) are what a method does to a single statistic — a blend of several rarely lands on them — and the same player can land on a different number under each method. (This hero's inverted scored stat — Deaths — is flipped first, so "better" always means a higher score; look for the ↓ in the Power Score table below.)

  1. Min-Max. Stretches the field so the lowest raw value becomes 0 and the highest becomes 100, with everyone else placed proportionally in between. Simple and intuitive, and the most sensitive of the three to how far apart the raw numbers actually are. One guard: in a pool of 12 or more, an endpoint sitting more than 50% of the pool's range away from its nearest neighbour is clamped onto the scale instead of defining it — otherwise that single value would flatten everyone else toward the middle — so it can end up sharing 0 (or 100) with the next player along.
  2. Z-Score. Measures how many standard deviations each player sits from the group's average, then maps that onto the 0–100 scale (50 = dead average). Resistant to outliers — one standout doesn't crush everyone else toward 0 the way it does under Min-Max — but that also means Z-Score rarely reaches the ends of the scale; an evenly-matched group clusters closer to the middle than Min-Max would show it.
  3. Percentile. Ranks players by raw value and converts each rank into "beat X% of the field" (ties share the average of their positions). Cares only about ORDER, not by how much: neighbouring players sit exactly 100 ÷ (players − 1) apart however close or far their raw numbers are — a 33-point step in a 4-player pool, under 3 points in a 36-player one.

The three are not three independent juries. All of them rescale the SAME stats, and Min-Max and Z-Score both read magnitude — on the current pools they produce the identical ranking on about six views in ten. Only Percentile, which reads order alone, is a structurally different question, so “unanimous” means the three rules agree, not that three independent measurements did.

With two players every stat collapses to 100 for whoever leads it and 0 for the other — where the two tie on a stat, to 100 for both under Min-Max but 50 for both under Percentile — so the Min-Max and Percentile composites only report which side holds the heavier-weighted stats; read Z-Score and the raw numbers for the real gap. With a single tracked player there is nothing to spread across at all: Min-Max and Percentile read 100 and Z-Score its neutral 50.

Projected competitive rank — Deadpool (Vanguard)

Projected Deadpool (Vanguard) rank — an estimate from how each player performs, not their live account rank. Take the order seriously at a distance rather than row by row: players a few places apart are a real difference, neighbouring rows usually sit inside the model's own margin, and the III/II/I sub-divisions are low-precision.

How are these ranks estimated?

The estimate deliberately ignores each player's real competitive rank — a rank can sit above or below true skill — and reads how they actually play instead. Three ingredients:

  1. A baseline from everything they play. Across all of a player's heroes, how much do they produce compared with the top of each hero's leaderboard? That sets their typical level — a hero with hundreds of games shapes it far more than a two-game cameo.
  2. This hero, against their own lobbies. In the group's recorded matches, does the player out-perform the other players in the same role in the same match — by more than a typical pilot of this hero does? Some kits out-stat a lobby just by existing (sustained-farm damage, high-uptime healing) and some under-stat it (dive, burst, utility), so each hero's lobby standing is judged against that hero's own typical number — the “typical for this hero” percentage in the row. Beating that pushes the hero above your baseline; falling short pushes it below. Competitive and Quick Match both count — the matchmaker pairs you against your own level either way — and recent games weigh more (a gentle half-life of 100 games), so growth shows without having to drag up a whole career's weight.
  3. A reality check. A hero can't read much higher than what the player actually puts up on it — beating an easy lobby on a hero you produce little on doesn't lift the tier. The check trusts itself in proportion to its evidence: a deep career's numbers cap the read almost absolutely, while a thin 15-game record only pulls it about halfway down (a handful of games shouldn't overrule hundreds of recorded lobbies). Reads held down this way are marked output-capped.

The confidence pill says how much match data backs the read on this hero: HIGH = a deep dominance sample, MED = a moderate one (hover it for the exact credibility score). Nobody's own Account Peak positions their own row — a player can read above it or below it, and where they land is decided entirely by the three ingredients above. Recorded ranks do set the board's scale, once, for everyone: the tier boundaries are fitted from the group's rank history, and because output-vs-benchmark runs systematically generous, the whole board is shifted so the median player lands on the median account peak. That is a ruler, not an anchor — it moves every row by the same amount and changes nobody's position in the order.

Where this read runs hot. The lobby comparison is only as strong as the lobbies, and nothing here subtracts how tough they were — a player who mostly meets weak ones can rest a tier or more above anything their account has actually reached. That is what the Account Peak column is for: a projection sitting far above a peak is the row to be sceptical of, not one the model has verified.

One all-time projection per player, not one per view: Quick Match and Competitive games both count, so this board covers every player shown in either view above — which can be more than either view lists on its own — and does not change when you switch modes.

How the radar is drawn

Each spoke is one scored statistic, and how far out a player sits is their 0–100 standing on that statistic within this group — never the raw number. The rim is the reference: 100 is the best value anyone in this group posts on that spoke, 0 is the worst, and the rings in between are unlabelled on purpose — hover a vertex for the standing it actually holds. The centre is not quite zero: the whole 0–100 range is drawn into the outer 92% of the radius, so a player at the bottom of a spoke still shows a short arm instead of collapsing onto the middle. Because that is a rescale and not a cut-off, two low standings keep different lengths here even where the floored bars in the tables below draw them level.

Statistics where fewer is better are turned around before they are plotted, so further out is better on every spoke whichever direction the statistic runs in. That is why the radar carries none of the ↓ marks the sortable columns do: the direction has already been taken out of the number being drawn.

Outlines rather than filled shapes, deliberately. A filled polygon reads as area, and area is not the Power Score — it counts every spoke alike and squares each one, while the score weights them (the formula bar at the top of this panel is those weights) and adds them straight. So a bigger outline is not always the better player. Identity rides the line instead: a colour per player plus a repeating dash pattern, so two overlapping players stay tellable apart without relying on colour, with the group leader drawn heaviest.

On a pool deeper than 10 players only the highest-ranked are drawn at first — the rest stay listed but dimmed — because every trace painting at once on a deep pool hides more than it shows. When that happens the chart grows a Draw all control, reachable from the keyboard, which turns the whole pool on and off. Smaller pools draw everybody.

How to read the bars

A bar is a standing, not the number beside it. Each statistic is rescaled 0–100 across the players shown — the group's best on that column fills the track, the group's worst sits at the floor below — and the bar draws that standing. It is what makes columns in different units comparable at a glance, and it is why a long bar means ahead of these players and never good in absolute terms: change the pool and every bar in the table moves. The Power column is the exception. Its bar is the composite score itself, drawn on a fixed 0–100 track rather than rescaled across the players shown — so unlike every other column here, the group's best Power bar fills the track only if that player actually scores 100, and on most panels it stops well short of the end.

The shortest bars are floored. A standing under 8 is drawn at 8 — and so is a Power score under 8, the one bar here that is not a standing — so a last place reads as a bar rather than a sliver, which means the shortest bars in a column can look level while the numbers beside them differ. Where two bars look the same length, the numbers are the answer.

Where the weights come from. Rescaling makes the statistics comparable; it does not make them equal. Each carries a fixed weight, set for this hero when its formula was calibrated — the composition bar at the top of this panel is those weights, drawn to scale and labelled with each share, with the calibration date beneath it. The Power column is exactly those weights applied to the standings in this table, which is why a player who leads more columns can still sit lower: the ones they lead may be the light ones.

How the ranking is built — and what it cannot tell you

A bare win-rate sort is a small-sample leaderboard, so every row is shrunk before it is ranked: its record is mixed with the rate this hero's whole qualifying pool posts, at a weight of 25 pseudo-games. A row therefore keeps 67% of its distance from that pool at the 50-game floor and 98% at the deepest sample in this mode (1,360 games). It is the same strength, and the same formula, the Record by map section ranks on. A pointer-only tooltip on each Adjusted rate adds the W–L record behind it, the pool it was pulled toward and the share of its own distance it keeps.

That is a nudge, not a guarantee: a short run far enough above the pool can still top the table, and it is the 50-game floor — not the shrink — that keeps a five-game hot streak off this page entirely. What the shrink settles is the common case, a just-past-the-floor record leading a deep one by a hair. Read the Games column beside any rate you are about to believe.

The table is sorted on the Adjusted column because that is what it ranks on, which means the raw Win % column can step up as you read down — it does on 36 of 499 adjacent pairs in this mode. That is deliberate: the alternative is a column whose order nobody can reproduce from the printed numbers. Click Games, Win % or the Adjusted heading to re-sort; Player is a label and # renumbers to the order you sort into.

Two things this cannot measure. The baseline in the last header is not a property of the hero — it is what these particular players happened to do on it, and it ranges 35.1%–60.6% across the 91 tables here, so two heroes' columns are not comparable. And a win is a team result: five other players decide it too, which is why the roster-wide effect the Matchups sections measure is large while the personal one is not. Power Ranking, above, is the composite of what a player individually put on the scoreboard; this is the record they were part of.

Team-ups — what wins with what

Which team-up Deadpool (Vanguard) wins with in quick match — 983 games since Season 9, the first season a hero could equip either of two team-ups, counting every player in the group's matches, not only the roster. Each card's win % is games won with that team-up equipped, over the games behind it; under 30 games it prints greyed, without a bar. It is a record, not a verdict — who picks a team-up, and what they play it into, moves the rate more than the team-up does, which is what the by-player rows hold fixed.

How this is counted

Since Season 9 every hero carries two team-ups and a player equips one, whether or not its named partner is on the team — so the equipped team-up is a per-player choice, read here from the match record (it is not in the tracker.gg data the rest of this page is built from). A game counts once per hero a player used, with the team-up they had on that hero; a win is that player's team winning the match. Bot lobbies and event modes are excluded, as everywhere on this page.

A card needs 30 games on a team-up to earn its bar and a player's cell 10; under that the rate still prints, greyed, with its wins–losses beside it, so a thin record reads as a thin record rather than vanishing. Neither rate is adjusted or ranked — these are records, and a team-up's population rate is mostly a fact about who chooses it. Every win-rate bar here is drawn on the same window around a coin flip as the map and teammate tables — the bar follows the printed rate, it just spends its width where win rates actually land. vs pool prints its margin of error beside it, and takes a colour only once the gap is larger than a family of that many comparisons could produce by luck — a muted bar is a real gap that is still inside the noise.

What 100% means here

100% is parity with the benchmark — the player is producing exactly what that statistic's elite reference figure says. Above it is better than the benchmark, below it is short of it. How the rate and its par are built

One number, two awards. The game gives exactly one award per six-player team — MVP to the standout on the winning side, SVP to the standout on the losing side — so folding them together answers a single question: how often is this player the best on their team when they run this hero? Because the SVP half counts, a player who has never taken an MVP on the hero can still print a real figure here.

Par is leave-one-out. It is what every other tracked player manages on this hero, with the row's own games and awards lifted out of the pool first, so nobody is graded against a figure they helped set. It is built from the hero's whole stored record rather than from the rows listed here, so it includes players the table's own floors leave off, and vs par is the subtraction of the two percentages printed beside it. Three consequences worth knowing: because par excludes the row, a player who owns most of a hero's record pushes their own par the other way as their rate moves, which stretches the printed gap; par carries no sample floor of its own, so where one player holds nearly the whole record the par beside them can rest on very few games; and par takes those pooled players' games as they fall, so their balance of quick-match against competitive play, and of one season against another, need not match the row's — a player whose split is unusual for this hero is measured against a slightly different mix of games than the one they played.

Two floors, and why. A row has to be qualified on this hero at the same game floors the rest of the panel uses, and hold 20+ captured matches on top of that — an award is a rarer event than a win, so a short run swings the rate far harder. Captured counts are floors of a player's true totals: the store holds the matches shared with tracked players, not every game they have ever played. The awards ride those same matches, so the rate stays honest even where the denominator is incomplete. How the gap is built

Every row's record is first pulled toward the pooled rate in the last column's heading, by its own sample size — the pull is worth 25 games, so a row sitting on this table's 10-game floor keeps only 29% of its distance from that pool while a 100-game row keeps 80% of it. The gap is that pulled rate minus the pooled rate, both at one decimal. The pooled rate is the last column's heading; the pulled rate is not a column — hover a gap for it, alongside the raw record it came from. Win % is deliberately left raw, and is not what the gap is taken from.

That pooled rate is not the hero's headline record, and the two are not meant to agree. These rows come from the Maps axis, which counts every tracked player who has played the hero; the ranking and career tables higher up the panel count only the players past the display floor. A wider population gives a different rate — neither is wrong, they answer different questions, and a gap here is only ever measured against the rate its own rows produced.

The bar a gap must clear scales with the table, not just with the row. How Lift is computed — and what it finds

Lift is the cell's win rate minus what the two heroes are worth apart: the group's rate over every teammate pairing it has ever recorded, plus how far this hero runs above that rate without that ally, plus how far that ally runs above it alongside every other hero. Each term leaves this pairing's own games out, and the gap is then pulled toward zero by the pairing's own sample — a 20-game run keeps 67% of its gap, a 2,000-game one 99.5%. That is a nudge, not a guard: a lucky short run can still top this table, and the ± beside it is what says so. That ± carries the error in the expectation as well as in the cell: the ally column is a few thousand games rather than a whole career, so its own uncertainty belongs in the band and is in it.

Why not just read Win %: it answers which teammate heroes are strong, not which pairings work. Remove one number per ally hero — the same number on all 56 heroes — and no measurable chemistry is left. On this build a page's Win % order agrees with that ally's own rate elsewhere at a mean rank correlation of +0.49; its Lift order, +0.02.

And the honest answer is that there is very little here. Across all 2,886 tested pairings on 56 tracked heroes, a Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery pass at 5% returns none. 4.8% of rows sit beyond ±2 of their own error where chance alone gives 4.6%, and the spread of the whole table's z scores is 1.01 against the 1.00 that pure noise produces. Once that noise is subtracted, the spread of real chemistry across the table is about 0.5 points — small enough that a simulated table built with zero chemistry in it reproduces the figure. Run it hero by hero instead — 56 smaller tests, so a lower bar — and 5 rows across 4 of the 56 heroes clear it, which is what 56 independent 5% tests turn up when nothing is there (about 3 heroes).

So the ranking is a shortlist of where to look, not a list of findings. Anything inside its own ± is even.

Players are ranked by their average finish across the heroes they play, each finish weighted by how much it's worth trusting: a deeper pool counts for more (#1 of 20 over #1 of 3, saturating around 7 opponents — past that, deeper fields in this group are not stronger ones), and a hero you've actually played counts for more than one that only just cleared the display floor. Finishing position itself is never rewritten by pool size — 7th of 36 is 7th of 36. That average is then pulled toward the middle of the board by how much evidence backs it, so breadth and volume decide who leads when two records are close — but a thin record is only ever held back from the top, never pushed below a worse one. The Avg finish chip on each profile is the raw, unadjusted figure. The standing itself is an exact place — players who score equally are separated by their average finish, so no two share one. A player carrying less than one fully-credible hero's worth of evidence is shown with a — instead of a place: not enough to rank them, which is not the same as a low rank.
